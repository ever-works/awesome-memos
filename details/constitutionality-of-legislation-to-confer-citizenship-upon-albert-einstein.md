## Overview

This memo, dated April 9, 1934, was written by J.T. Fowler of the Department of Justice in response to a proposal to grant citizenship to physicist Albert Einstein through special legislation. The memo examines the constitutional basis for such an act, focusing on the Uniform Naturalization Clause.

## Context

In the early 1930s, Einstein, a German-Jewish refugee fleeing Nazi persecution, was offered citizenship by various countries. The memo discusses whether Congress could pass a law specifically naturalizing Einstein, or whether such an act would violate the constitutional requirement for a uniform rule of naturalization.

## Key Points
- The memo argues that the uniformity requirement in Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution refers to geographical uniformity, not uniformity in the persons naturalized.
- It concludes that Congress has the sole power to determine the rules of naturalization and may therefore grant citizenship to Einstein via legislation.
- However, the memo cautions that doing so "may be unwise" due to potential perceptions of favoritism.

## Historical Significance

Although Einstein ultimately became a U.S. citizen through the standard naturalization process in 1940, this memo reflects early debates about the scope of congressional power over immigration and citizenship, and the tension between uniform rules and humanitarian discretion.

## Excerpt

"In other words, Congress has the sole power to determine the requisites of citizenship by naturalization, and to determine even more specifically who may or who may not be admitted to such citizenship."
