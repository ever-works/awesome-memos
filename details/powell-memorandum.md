# Powell Memorandum

## Overview

The Powell Memorandum, officially titled "Attack on American Free Enterprise System", is a confidential memorandum dated August 23, 1971, written by Lewis F. Powell Jr. (who would later be appointed to the U.S. Supreme Court) for Mr. Eugene B. Sydnor Jr., Chairman of the Education Committee of the U.S. Chamber of Commerce.

## Context

Written during a period of social unrest and growing criticism of corporate power, the memo responded to perceived threats to the American free enterprise system from various quarters including college campuses, media outlets, intellectual journals, and politicians. Powell characterized these attacks as "broadly based and consistently pursued" rather than isolated extremist actions.

## Key Findings

- Identified diverse sources of criticism including Communists, New Leftists, college campuses, pulpits, media, intellectual journals, arts and sciences, and politicians
- Noted that while extremists were a small minority, "respectable" elements of society were increasingly joining the criticism
- Highlighted the irony that institutions criticizing the system (universities, media) were often funded by or dependent on business
- Cited specific examples including William Kunstler's advocacy of street fighting, attacks on Bank of America branches, and Ralph Nader's consumer activism
- Referenced Charles Reich's book "The Greening of America" and student polls showing support for socializing basic industries

## Recommended Actions

The memo proposed a coordinated business response including:

1. **Staff of Scholars**: Establishing a team of reputable scholars who believe in the free enterprise system to counter intellectual criticism
2. **Speaker's Bureau**: Creating a bureau of effective advocates from top American business to balance campus speaking circuits
3. **Textbook Evaluation**: Having scholars review social science textbooks for balanced treatment of the American system
4. **Equal Time**: Insisting on equal access to college speaking forums for pro-free enterprise viewpoints
5. **Faculty Balance**: Urging university administrators to achieve better political balance among faculty
6. **Graduate Business Schools**: Developing specific courses on defending the free enterprise system
7. **Secondary Education**: Extending similar efforts to high schools
8. **Public Outreach**: Using television, radio, press, scholarly journals, books, and paid advertising to present the affirmative case for free enterprise
9. **Political Engagement**: Increasing business influence in political processes through lobbying and civic engagement

## Historical Significance

The Powell Memorandum is widely regarded as a foundational document that helped organize and energize business political activism in the 1970s and beyond. It influenced the creation of numerous business-funded think tanks, policy organizations, and advocacy groups designed to counter perceived threats to free enterprise principles. The memo's emphasis on intellectual counteraction, rather than purely defensive public relations, marked a shift in how American business approached societal debates about its role and legitimacy.

## Legacy

While Powell maintained that the memo was merely advisory and not a call to action, many of its recommendations were subsequently implemented by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce and allied business organizations. The document is frequently cited in discussions about the history of corporate political strategy, the rise of conservative think tanks, and the evolving relationship between American business and society in the late 20th century.
